# SECTION 1 — THINK ON PAPER

# VIDEO 1.1
## The Problem With Thinking Inside Your Head

### Target duration
7–9 minutes

### Core idea

Business thinking becomes easier to challenge when it becomes visible.

### SCRIPT

Think about the last time you had what felt like a really good business idea.

You probably played it out in your head.

You could already see the customer.

You could already imagine the product.

You could already see the revenue.

And because the story was running inside your head, it probably sounded very convincing.

[PAUSE]

That's the problem.

Your mind is extremely good at turning fragments into a coherent story.

But coherence is not the same as accuracy.

Let me give you a simple example.

Imagine a founder says:

> “Our customers are asking for this.”

Fine.

But what actually happened?

Maybe one customer mentioned something in a meeting.

Maybe another customer complained about a related issue.

And the founder connected the dots.

So now we have three different things:

What was observed.

What was inferred.

What is being assumed.

Those are not the same thing.

[ON SCREEN]

**OBSERVATION**

What happened?

**INTERPRETATION**

What do I think it means?

**ASSUMPTION**

What am I treating as true?

This distinction sounds basic.

It is not basic in practice.

It is one of the most important disciplines in business thinking.

Because once observation, interpretation and assumption collapse into one sentence, they become very hard to challenge.

For example:

> “Customers want an AI dashboard.”

That sounds clear.

But let's slow it down.

What happened?

Maybe customers complained that reports were slow.

What did we interpret?

That they need a dashboard.

What did we assume?

That an AI dashboard is the right solution.

Now we have surfaced the thinking.

And once we can see the thinking, we can challenge it.

That is why I want you to work on paper.

Not because paper is magical.

Because visible thinking is inspectable thinking.

[PAUSE]

Take your signal now.

And write three things.

What happened?

What do you think it means?

And what are you assuming?

Don't make it beautiful.

Make it honest.

### Memory line

> **Thinking becomes easier when you can see it.**

### Learner task

# VIDEO 1.2
## Why Structured Notes Matter

### Target duration
8–10 minutes

### Core idea

Separate the components of business thinking.

### SCRIPT

Now that you have your signal on paper, let's make one more move.

A useful business thought usually contains several different things at once.

There's the situation.

There's the problem.

There's your interpretation.

There are alternatives.

There are assumptions.

There are implications.

And there is some action you are considering.

The danger is that we compress all of that into one sentence.

For example:

> “We should launch this because customers need it.”

That's a very compressed thought.

Let's unpack it.

What is the situation?

Customers are doing something.

What is the problem?

Something is not working as well as it could.

What is the interpretation?

We think we know why.

What are the alternatives?

There may be other ways to solve it.

What are the assumptions?

We are taking certain things for granted.

What are the implications?

If our view is right, something valuable may be possible.

What is the action?

We want to launch something.

Once you separate those pieces, the quality of thinking improves.

You can challenge one part without destroying the whole thought.

[ON SCREEN]

> **Do not let observation, interpretation and assumption become one sentence.**

This is especially important with your signal.

Take what you originally wrote.

Now rewrite it as:

One: observation.

Two: interpretation.

Three: assumption.

And don't worry if your assumption becomes less convincing.

That's actually useful.

The goal is not to protect the idea.

The goal is to make the idea thinkable.

### Memory line

> **Do not let observation, interpretation and assumption become one sentence.**

### Learner task

Create the **Structured Signal Record**.

## Why Decisions Need Records

### Target duration
6–8 minutes

### Core idea

A decision should be inspectable after it is made.

### SCRIPT

Most organizations remember decisions badly.

They remember the conclusion.

They forget the reasoning.

Someone says:

> “We decided to go after this market.”

Six months later, nobody remembers exactly why.

What did we know then?

What did we not know?

What alternatives did we consider?

What made this choice attractive?

What evidence were we missing?

Those things disappear.

And that makes learning much harder.

A useful decision record preserves the thinking around the decision.

[ON SCREEN]

- What was known?
- What was assumed?
- What alternatives existed?
- Why was the choice made?
- What evidence was missing?

This doesn't have to be a fifty-page strategy report.

It can be a small record.

Situation.

Choice.

Reasoning.

Unknowns.

Next step.

That's enough to make the decision inspectable.

And there is a deeper reason this matters.

If a decision can survive only inside the memory of the person who made it, the organization has not really learned.

[PAUSE]

So take one current business decision.

It doesn't have to be the biggest decision in your company.

Record it.

Situation.

Choice.

Reasoning.

Unknowns.

Next step.

You may be surprised by how much becomes visible once you write it down.

### Target duration
8–10 minutes

### SCRIPT

Now we can ask:

> **What is the smallest meaningful way to learn?**

Not:

> What is the biggest research project we can run?

> How can we validate the entire market?

And not:

> What test does everyone else use?

The question is:

> What do we most need to learn…

and…

> what is the smallest meaningful way to learn it?

Depending on the situation, that could be:

An observation.

An interview.

A customer conversation.

A prototype.

A pretotype.

A small experiment.

A landing page.

A mock sale.

Or another proportionate learning action.

The right test depends on the important unknown.

Suppose you are assuming customers will pay.

Don't ask them:

> “Would you buy this?”

Try to design something that gives you more meaningful evidence.

Suppose you're assuming operators will use a new process.

Observe the workflow.

Suppose you're assuming a concept is understandable.

Show a prototype.

The test should be linked to the assumption that matters most.

[PAUSE]

So now define:

What do we need to learn?

Who or what do we need to test?

What action will generate learning?

And what result would change our thinking?

> “I have an idea.”

> “Here is the opportunity.”

An idea is private.

An opportunity can be explained.

A credible opportunity should contain:

The situation.

What changed.

The opportunity that change creates.

Why it matters.

Why this choice deserves attention.

What we believe.

What remains uncertain.

And what we propose to test next.

[ON SCREEN]

Can they understand what changed?
