## Cases 02–04
### Full end-to-end case specifications at the same rigour as Case 01
### Version 1.0

# How to Use This Library

Cases 02–04 are designed to sit alongside:

> **Case 01 — Indian Deep-Tech Startup / New Product Launch**

Together the four cases stress four materially different uncertainty environments:

1. **Opportunity / commercial uncertainty** — new product launch
2. **Causal / customer-problem uncertainty** — existing customer problem
3. **Technical / operational uncertainty** — technology problem
4. **External-system / regulatory uncertainty** — policy or regulation problem

The purpose is not to teach four sectors.

The purpose is to demonstrate that the same CORE reasoning can select **different evidence-generation approaches** depending on the nature of the decision and uncertainty.

The underlying principle is:

> **CORE standardizes the reasoning for evidence, not the evidence-generation method.**

---

# CASE 02
# EXISTING CUSTOMER PROBLEM

## 1. Case Purpose

This case demonstrates CORE when the client does **not** begin with a new product.

The client has an existing business, existing customers and a measurable commercial problem.

Management already has a diagnosis.

CORE must determine whether that diagnosis is actually supported.

The intended transformation is:

> **Symptom → Diagnosis → Evidence → Intervention → Decision**

The case is deliberately designed so that a conventional “validation test” is **not automatically appropriate**.

---

# 2. Starting Situation

## The Company

A hypothetical Indian industrial equipment manufacturer sells machinery and aftermarket services to established B2B customers.

Over the last 18 months:

- repeat orders have declined in an important customer segment;
- several accounts have become less active;
- sales teams report increasing competitive pressure.

Management's initial view is:

> **“Customers are leaving because competitors are cheaper.”**

The management team wants a recommendation on what to do.

---

# 3. The Initial Problem Statement

> **Our prices are too high and customers are switching to lower-cost competitors.**

This is an explanation, not a demonstrated fact.

CORE does not treat it as the problem until it has been examined.

---

# 4. CORE Step 1 — Diagnose the Problem

## Situation

The observable situation is:

> **Repeat business has declined.**

That is what happened.

---

## Interpretation

Management interprets the decline as:

> **Price-driven competitive loss.**

That interpretation may or may not be correct.

---

# 5. Alternative Problem Frames

## Frame A — Price

> Customers are leaving because our total commercial offer is less attractive than competitors'.

---

## Frame B — Service

> Customers are reducing repeat business because our service responsiveness has deteriorated.

---

## Frame C — Reliability

> Customers are leaving because product or service reliability is creating operational problems.

---

## Frame D — Requirements

> Customers' needs have changed and our current offering no longer fits them.

---

## Frame E — Relationship / Process

> Customers are leaving because account-management, procurement or service processes have become harder to work with.

---

## Frame F — Market Structure

> Competitors are changing the buying environment rather than simply offering a lower price.

---

# 6. Why the Frames Matter

Each frame creates a different intervention.

If the problem is price:

> pricing / commercial redesign.

If service:

> service system redesign.

If reliability:

> engineering / product intervention.

If requirements:

> product / market repositioning.

If relationship/process:

> commercial operating redesign.

Therefore:

> **Diagnosing the cause matters before selecting the intervention.**

---

# 7. Desired Outcome

The company does not simply want:

> “More repeat orders.”

The immediate desired outcome is:

> **Identify the dominant, decision-relevant drivers of repeat-order decline and determine what intervention deserves investment.**

---

# 8. Decision at Stake

> **Should the company change its customer-retention strategy for this segment, and if so, what should it change first?**

This is the decision evidence must support.

---

# 9. What Do We Need to Know?

Potential questions:

### Customer behaviour

> What changed in customer ordering behaviour?

### Cause

> Why did the change occur?

### Price

> How important is price relative to other factors?

### Service

> Is service responsiveness affecting retention?

### Reliability

> Is reliability influencing repeat purchases?

### Requirements

> Have customers' needs changed?

### Competition

> What are competitors doing differently?

The questions are broader than:

> “Do customers think we're expensive?”

---

# 10. What Do We Already Know?

Before interviews, examine:

## Internal evidence

- order history
- customer retention by account
- quotation history
- lost-order reasons
- discounting records
- service response times
- complaint records
- warranty claims
- delivery performance
- product failure data
- CRM notes
- account-manager observations

## External evidence

Potentially:

- competitor offerings
- market developments
- industry conditions
- customer business changes

---

# 11. Hypothetical Evidence Review

Suppose the internal review finds:

- repeat-order decline is concentrated in accounts with high service-contact frequency;
- many lost orders had no recorded price objection;
- several accounts experienced long service response times;
- discounting did not clearly restore repeat orders in affected accounts.

This does not prove that service is the cause.

But it weakens:

> **“Price alone explains the decline.”**

---

# 12. Evidence Status

| Condition | Status | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Repeat orders have declined | **Known** | Internal transaction evidence |
| Some customers mention price | **Indicated** | Requires interpretation |
| Price is the main cause | **Assumed** | Not adequately established |
| Service responsiveness has deteriorated | **Indicated** | Internal records suggest a pattern |
| Service deterioration causes retention loss | **Unknown** | Requires further evidence |
| Customer requirements have changed | **Unknown** | Requires evidence |
| Competitors are structurally better | **Unknown** | Requires evidence |

---

# 13. CORE Step 2 — Identify the Decision Need

The decision is:

> **What should the company change first to improve retention?**

The research question therefore becomes:

> **Which factors are most strongly associated with repeat-order loss in the affected segment, and how do customers describe their relative importance?**

This is materially better than:

> “Do customers think our price is high?”

---

# 14. CORE Step 3 — Critical Uncertainty

Suppose the evidence review suggests the most consequential unresolved issue is:

> **Whether service responsiveness, rather than price, is a major driver of repeat-order decline.**

This becomes the critical uncertainty.

---

# 15. Critical Barrier

The decision could be materially distorted if management invests in price reductions while the real problem lies elsewhere.

The critical barrier is:

> **The company may choose the wrong intervention because it has mistaken a symptom or common complaint for the principal cause.**

---

# 16. What Evidence Do We Need?

We need evidence that can distinguish between:

- price;
- service;
- reliability;
- changing requirements;
- relationship/process;
- other causes.

Evidence should ideally connect:

> **actual account behaviour**

with:

> **customer explanation**

and:

> **operational records.**

---

# 17. Candidate Evidence Methods

## Method A — Internal Data Analysis

Can establish:

- patterns of repeat ordering;
- service-response patterns;
- relationship between account characteristics and retention.

Cannot alone establish:

> why the customer chose to leave.

---

## Method B — Customer Interviews

Can establish:

- customer experience;
- decision criteria;
- reasons for change;
- context.

Cannot automatically establish:

> causal importance across the entire segment.

---

## Method C — Account-Manager Interviews

Can reveal:

- account history;
- competitor information;
- unusual events;
- customer behaviour.

Limitation:

> internal perspective can contain interpretation bias.

---

## Method D — Service / Operational Record Analysis

Can establish:

- response time;
- complaint patterns;
- reliability;
- operational incidents.

Cannot alone establish:

> how customers weigh those factors against price.

---

## Method E — Observation / Account Review

Can reveal:

- actual process friction;
- workflow;
- handoffs;
- recurring operational issues.

May be expensive and context-specific.

---

# 18. Method Selection

A reasonable initial evidence-generation design is:

> **Internal evidence analysis → targeted customer interviews → account/service evidence triangulation.**

Why?

Because the critical uncertainty is **causal and diagnostic**, not simply commercial willingness.

An experiment is not automatically warranted.

---

# 19. Why This Method?

## Directness

It addresses the underlying cause of retention change.

## Credibility

It combines:

> behavioural history + customer experience + operational evidence.

## Speed

Existing data can narrow the investigation before interviews.

## Cost

Targeted interviews are more proportionate than a large market study.

## Limitation

The design may still not prove a universal causal relationship.

The objective is:

> **decision-relevant diagnosis**, not academic causal proof.

---

# 20. Evidence Standard

The company is deciding:

> what intervention deserves priority.

Therefore the evidence should be strong enough to distinguish the leading explanations sufficiently for that strategic choice.

The standard is not:

> statistical certainty.

The standard is:

> **sufficiently convergent evidence to make a defensible intervention decision.**

---

# 21. Acceptance Criteria

For this diagnostic research design:

> **No universal technical acceptance criterion is required.**

The relevant criterion is whether the evidence is sufficiently coherent and decision-useful to distinguish the leading explanations.

This is an example of:

> **Acceptance Criteria — Not Applicable in the technical-test sense.**

---

# 22. Decision Rule

### GO — Service intervention

If:

- account history;
- customer evidence;
- operational evidence

converge on service responsiveness as a material driver.

### MODIFY

If service matters but is not the only significant driver.

### STOP

If the service hypothesis is repeatedly unsupported.

### TEST AGAIN

If customer accounts provide contradictory explanations or the evidence sources disagree materially.

---

# 23. Hypothetical Execution

Suppose the company reviews 40 affected accounts.

Patterns show:

- 25 accounts experienced service-response issues;
- 18 mention response delays when discussing lost business;
- 8 primarily cite price;
- some accounts have both issues;
- several accounts changed requirements unrelated to either.

The evidence is not:

> “Service caused 100% of churn.”

It is:

> **Service responsiveness appears to be a significant and actionable contributor, while price and changing requirements also matter in subsets of accounts.**

---

# 24. Interpretation

Correct interpretation:

> **The original price-only diagnosis is not sufficiently supported. Service responsiveness appears to deserve immediate strategic attention, but the intervention should not ignore heterogeneous account causes.**

Incorrect interpretation:

> “Customers don't care about price.”

The evidence does not establish that.

---

# 25. Learning Card

## We believed that...

> Customers were leaving primarily because our price was too high.

## We observed...

> The strongest recurring evidence also pointed to service responsiveness and differing account circumstances.

## From that we learned...

> The retention problem is not adequately explained by price alone.

## What changed?

> We now need a segmented retention intervention rather than a blanket price reduction.

## What remains uncertain?

> Which specific service/process changes would produce the greatest retention improvement.

---

# 26. Decision

> **MODIFY**

The company should redesign its retention intervention around:

- service responsiveness;
- account segmentation;
- account-specific causes;
- continued monitoring of price sensitivity.

---

# 27. Next Evidence Generation

The next evidence question becomes:

> **Will improving service responsiveness materially change customer behaviour?**

Now an operational intervention or controlled trial may become appropriate.

Notice:

> **The first evidence activity was not the final test.**

It changed the question.

That is exactly the behaviour CORE should teach.

---

# 28. What This Case Teaches

### Lesson 1

> **A business symptom is not automatically the cause.**

### Lesson 2

> **Management's explanation is a hypothesis, not evidence.**

### Lesson 3

> **Existing evidence can be the first test of an assumption.**

### Lesson 4

> **Different evidence sources can triangulate a diagnosis.**

### Lesson 5

> **Not every strategic problem requires an experiment.**

### Lesson 6

> **Evidence can change the problem definition itself.**

---

# CASE 03
# TECHNOLOGY PROBLEM

## 32. Case Purpose

This case tests CORE when the main uncertainty is **technical and operational**, rather than primarily customer demand.

It demonstrates that:

> **technical feasibility requires technical evidence.**

The case also tests the distinction between:

> **Acceptance Criteria**

and:

> **Evidence Standard.**

---

# 33. Starting Situation

## The Company

A hypothetical Indian deep-tech company has developed an industrial sensor platform intended to monitor machine performance and detect early signs of failure.

The technology works in controlled laboratory conditions.

The commercial opportunity depends on deploying it in older manufacturing plants.

The founder says:

> **“The technology works. We just need customers.”**

CORE does not accept that statement.

---

# 34. The Initial Problem Statement

> **We need to convince manufacturers to buy our sensor platform.**

This may be premature.

The real question may be:

> **Can the platform operate reliably enough in representative customer environments to create the promised value?**

---

# 35. Problem Diagnosis

Potential uncertainties:

- connectivity;
- machine compatibility;
- data quality;
- installation;
- environmental conditions;
- signal interference;
- operator interaction;
- maintenance;
- cybersecurity;
- reliability;
- integration cost.

---

# 36. Desired Outcome

> **Demonstrate that the technology can reliably generate the required operational information in representative customer environments without unacceptable deployment burden.**

---

# 37. Decision at Stake

> **Should the company commit significant resources to commercial deployment in this manufacturing environment?**

---

# 38. What Do We Need to Know?

Questions include:

### Performance

Does the system meet required accuracy?

### Reliability

Does it remain stable over the operating period?

### Compatibility

Can it work with representative machines?

### Integration

Can it connect to available infrastructure?

### Environment

Does it perform under actual operating conditions?

### Operations

Can plant personnel use and maintain it?

### Economics

Does the deployment remain viable after integration requirements are included?

---

# 39. What Do We Already Know?

### Existing technical evidence

- laboratory performance;
- bench testing;
- prototype results;
- known failure modes.

### Existing customer evidence

- early discussions;
- installation feedback where available;
- technical constraints.

### Existing environment evidence

- machine specifications;
- interface requirements;
- plant operating conditions.

---

# 40. Evidence Status

| Condition | Status | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Technology performs in controlled lab conditions | **Known / evidenced within case premise** | Lab capability established |
| Technology performs in representative plants | **Unknown** | Critical gap |
| Legacy equipment can provide required data | **Unknown** | Critical gap |
| Installation effort is acceptable | **Unknown** | Important |
| Operators can use the system | **Unknown** | Important |
| Reliability is sufficient for operational use | **Unknown** | Critical |
| Economics remain attractive after integration | **Unknown** | Critical |

---

# 41. WWHTBT

For the deployment choice, relevant conditions might include:

1. system meets required technical performance;
2. representative equipment can be connected;
3. data quality is adequate;
4. performance remains stable over required duration;
5. environmental conditions are tolerable;
6. installation burden is acceptable;
7. operators can work with the system;
8. maintenance requirements are manageable;
9. deployment economics remain viable.

---

# 42. Critical Uncertainty

Suppose the biggest unknown is:

> **Can the platform reliably produce usable data from representative brownfield equipment under real operating conditions?**

---

# 43. Critical Barrier

> **If the system cannot reliably integrate and operate in representative environments, the commercial deployment model may not be viable.**

This could invalidate the investment case even if the laboratory technology is excellent.

---

# 44. Evidence Required

The required evidence is:

> **Measured performance under representative operating conditions.**

This is materially different from:

> “Plant managers like it.”

---

# 45. Candidate Methods

## Method A — Customer Interviews

Can establish:

- environment;
- equipment constraints;
- operating requirements.

Cannot establish:

> actual technical performance.

---

## Method B — Laboratory Test

Can establish:

- controlled performance.

Cannot establish:

> behaviour in the customer's environment.

---

## Method C — Representative Equipment Test

Can establish:

- compatibility;
- performance;
- data quality;
- integration.

---

## Method D — Field Trial

Can establish:

- real operating performance;
- environmental effects;
- operator interaction;
- reliability.

But it requires more time and access.

---

## Method E — Pilot

Can integrate:

- technical;
- operational;
- commercial evidence.

It is more substantial and should have a clear learning objective.

---

# 46. Method Selection

Suppose representative equipment is available before a full customer deployment.

Selected method:

> **Representative-equipment test followed by a limited field evaluation if the technical threshold is met.**

This is a two-stage evidence design.

The second stage is conditional.

---

# 47. Why This Method?

## Directness

It directly tests the critical technical uncertainty.

## Credibility

Evidence is produced under conditions closer to deployment.

## Proportionality

It avoids jumping directly to a broad commercial rollout.

## Escalation logic

The larger field commitment is earned by the earlier technical evidence.

---

# 48. Acceptance Criteria

Here acceptance criteria are essential.

Define, for example:

### Performance

Required detection/measurement performance.

### Data quality

Minimum acceptable completeness/quality.

### Reliability

Maximum acceptable failure/interruption rate.

### Duration

Required continuous operating period.

### Integration

Maximum acceptable integration burden.

### Environment

Required operating range.

The actual thresholds must be defined by the specific technical application.

CORE must not invent universal thresholds.

---

# 49. Evidence Standard

Acceptance criteria answer:

> **Did the technology meet the defined technical requirements?**

Evidence standard answers:

> **Is the evidence sufficiently reliable for the commercial deployment decision?**

For a significant deployment decision, one successful demonstration may be insufficient.

The decision may require:

- representative environments;
- repeatability;
- sufficient operating time;
- independent confirmation;
- economic assessment.

The exact standard is decision-dependent.

---

# 50. Decision Rule

### GO

Technical acceptance criteria are met with sufficient evidence and deployment economics remain viable.

### MODIFY

Technical capability is promising but:

- integration;
- environment;
- economics;
- workflow

require changes.

### STOP

A critical technical requirement repeatedly fails.

### TEST AGAIN

Conditions were not representative or evidence is inconsistent.

### ESCALATE

Evidence is strong enough to justify a field pilot or larger commercial commitment.

---

# 51. Hypothetical Execution

The system is tested on three representative machine environments.

### Environment A

Performance meets criteria.

### Environment B

Data quality is insufficient.

### Environment C

Installation requires unexpected integration work.

The technology has not “failed.”

But the commercial deployment model has revealed a critical issue.

---

# 52. Interpretation

Correct interpretation:

> **The core technology is promising, but the deployment architecture is not yet sufficiently robust for broad rollout. Integration and data-quality conditions must be addressed before significant commercial commitment.**

Incorrect interpretation:

> “The technology works because two machines passed.”

That would overstate the evidence.

---

# 53. Learning Card

## We believed that...

> The laboratory-proven sensor platform could be deployed with limited modification.

## We observed...

> Representative environments exposed data-quality and integration requirements that were not visible in laboratory testing.

## From that we learned...

> Deployment feasibility is a distinct uncertainty from laboratory performance.

## What changed?

> The product/deployment proposition must include the integration layer.

## What remains uncertain?

> Whether the modified deployment model can achieve acceptable economics and repeatability.

---

# 54. Decision

> **MODIFY + ESCALATE CONDITIONALLY**

The company should improve:

- integration;
- data handling;
- installation process;

and then conduct the next level of field evidence generation.

---

# 55. What This Case Teaches

### Lesson 1

> **Technology that works in a laboratory may still fail in deployment.**

### Lesson 2

> **Technical uncertainty needs technical evidence.**

### Lesson 3

> **Acceptance criteria are not the same as evidence standards.**

### Lesson 4

> **Representative conditions matter.**

### Lesson 5

> **A larger test should be earned by earlier evidence when appropriate.**

---

# CASE 04
# POLICY / REGULATORY PROBLEM

## 59. Case Purpose

This case tests CORE when the uncertainty is driven by:

- policy;
- regulation;
- institutional change;
- external rules.

The key learning is:

> **Not every uncertainty is experimentally testable.**

Sometimes the first evidence required is:

> **authoritative information + expert interpretation + affected-stakeholder evidence.**

---

# 60. Starting Situation

## The Company

A hypothetical Indian industrial manufacturer discovers that a new policy/regulatory requirement may affect one of its major product categories.

Management immediately says:

> **“We need to redesign the product to comply.”**

CORE does not accept this as the final problem.

---

# 61. Initial Problem Statement

> **The new regulation will make our product uncompetitive.**

This is a hypothesis.

---

# 62. Problem Diagnosis

Potential questions:

- Is the product actually covered?
- What requirement changed?
- When does it apply?
- Are there exclusions or exemptions?
- Is the interpretation settled?
- What changes operationally?
- What will customers have to do?
- What competitors face the same issue?
- Could compliance create a new market?
- Is the threat actually an opportunity?

---

# 63. Alternative Frames

## Frame A — Compliance Cost

> The regulation creates additional product/process cost.

## Frame B — Market Disruption

> The regulation changes competitive conditions.

## Frame C — Customer Burden

> Customers will need new compliance capabilities.

## Frame D — Market Opportunity

> New compliance requirements create demand for new products/services.

## Frame E — Strategic Timing

> The advantage depends on acting before competitors or customers adapt.

---

# 64. Desired Outcome

> **Determine how the external change affects the business and what strategic response deserves commitment.**

---

# 65. Decision at Stake

> **Should we modify the product, change the market strategy, or build a new compliance-related opportunity?**

---

# 66. What Do We Need to Know?

Potential questions:

### Regulatory fact

> What does the rule actually require?

### Applicability

> Which products/customers are covered?

### Timing

> When does the requirement become relevant?

### Interpretation

> How should ambiguous provisions be understood?

### Customer impact

> What will affected customers have to change?

### Competitive impact

> What will competitors face?

### Opportunity

> Does the rule create new customer demand?

---

# 67. What Do We Already Know?

## Authoritative evidence

- regulation;
- official notification;
- government guidance;
- applicable standards.

## Internal evidence

- affected SKUs;
- product specifications;
- customer base;
- compliance status.

## External evidence

- industry interpretation;
- competitor response;
- customer communications;
- credible technical material.

---

# 68. Evidence Status

| Condition | Status | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| A policy/regulatory change exists | **Known** | Established by authoritative source |
| Our product is definitely covered | **To establish** | Requires interpretation |
| Effective date is clear | **To establish** | Requires authoritative evidence |
| Customer compliance burden will increase | **Unknown** | Requires analysis/evidence |
| Competitors face the same impact | **Unknown** | Requires market evidence |
| New demand will arise | **Unknown** | Requires customer/market evidence |

---

# 69. Critical Uncertainty

Suppose the regulatory applicability itself is established.

The critical commercial uncertainty becomes:

> **Will the regulatory change create a sufficiently large and urgent customer need to justify a new compliance-related offering?**

This is now a market/opportunity question created by the regulatory situation.

---

# 70. Critical Barrier

> **Management may interpret a regulatory change as a market threat without determining whether affected customers will actually experience sufficient burden to pay for a solution.**

---

# 71. Evidence Required

Evidence needs to establish two different things:

### Regulatory reality

> What is actually required?

### Market consequence

> What do affected customers actually need to do, and what value would a solution create?

These require different evidence sources.

---

# 72. Candidate Evidence Methods

## Method A — Authoritative Regulatory Research

Establishes:

- rule;
- scope;
- dates;
- requirements.

Cannot establish:

> customer willingness to pay.

---

## Method B — Regulatory / Technical Expert Interpretation

Useful for:

- ambiguous provisions;
- implementation implications;
- industry practice.

Cannot establish:

> customer demand.

---

## Method C — Affected-Customer Interviews

Useful for:

- current understanding;
- implementation burden;
- process changes;
- emerging needs.

Weakness:

> stated demand is not commercial commitment.

---

## Method D — Competitor / Market Analysis

Useful for:

- response patterns;
- emerging offers;
- market positioning.

Cannot prove:

> customers will buy.

---

## Method E — Commercial Signal

Examples:

- requests for support;
- request for quotation;
- paid assessment;
- paid compliance service;
- other meaningful commercial action.

Useful for:

> willingness to act.

---

# 73. Method Selection

Hypothetical sequence:

> **Authoritative regulatory research → expert interpretation → affected-customer evidence → commercial signal**

The sequence is conditional.

If authoritative research alone resolves the issue:

> stop there.

If the rule is clear but customer impact is uncertain:

> proceed to affected customers.

If demand appears real:

> generate commercial evidence.

---

# 74. Why This Method?

It separates:

> **rule**

from:

> **interpretation**

from:

> **business consequence**

from:

> **commercial response.**

This prevents management from collapsing all four into:

> “Regulation = opportunity.”

---

# 75. Acceptance Criteria

Technical acceptance criteria may be:

> **Not applicable to the initial regulatory research stage.**

For any resulting product/compliance solution, technical or regulatory acceptance criteria may become necessary later.

The initial question is primarily:

> **What is required and what commercial consequence follows?**

---

# 76. Evidence Standard

The evidence standard depends on the decision.

For a preliminary strategic response:

> credible authoritative evidence + sufficiently convergent customer impact evidence

may be appropriate.

For a major product redesign:

> substantially stronger regulatory, technical and commercial evidence would be required.

---

# 77. Decision Rule

### GO

Evidence indicates:

- regulatory impact is real;
- customer burden is meaningful;
- strategic opportunity exists;
- commercial response is credible.

### MODIFY

The opportunity exists but requires a different target, proposition or delivery model.

### STOP

The supposed opportunity is unsupported.

### TEST AGAIN

Regulatory interpretation or market response remains materially ambiguous.

### DEFER

The external requirement is clear but the commercial impact is too uncertain to justify immediate commitment.

---

# 78. Hypothetical Execution

Suppose:

### Regulatory research

Confirms the new requirement affects the target product.

### Expert interpretation

Clarifies the implementation burden.

### Customer conversations

Find that affected customers are struggling to understand compliance requirements.

### Commercial signal

Several customers request a structured compliance assessment.

This is stronger than:

> “Customers say compliance is important.”

It shows movement toward actual demand.

---

# 79. Interpretation

Correct:

> **The regulatory change creates a credible customer problem and an emerging commercial opportunity, but the scale and repeatability of demand still need further evidence.**

Incorrect:

> “The regulation guarantees a new market.”

# 80. Learning Card

## We believed that...

> The regulation would simply increase compliance cost and threaten our product.

## We observed...

> Customers are facing a new compliance burden that may create demand for support and solutions.

## From that we learned...

> The external change may be both a threat and an opportunity.

## What changed?

> We should investigate a compliance-related offering rather than only redesigning the existing product.

## What remains uncertain?

> Whether the demand is large, repeatable and commercially attractive.

> **MODIFY**

Shift from:

> pure defensive compliance response

toward:

> **dual strategy — protect the existing product + explore the emerging compliance opportunity.**

# 86. Four-Case Synthesis

The four cases produce four different evidence architectures.

| Case | Nature of uncertainty | First evidence | Likely evidence generation | Experiment mandatory? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **01 New Product** | Opportunity / commercial | Existing technical + customer evidence | Commercial/technical evaluation, buyer action | No |
| **02 Customer Problem** | Causal / diagnostic | Internal evidence | Data + targeted interviews + operational evidence | No |
| **03 Technology Problem** | Technical / operational | Existing technical evidence | Representative technical test / field evaluation | Often |
| **04 Regulation** | External-system / commercial | Authoritative evidence | Regulation + expert + customer + commercial signal | No |

# 87. What the Four Cases Prove

Case 02.

All four.

All four.

## A test can be absent.

Cases 02 and 04 may not require an experiment.

Case 03.

# 89. Teaching Design for Graphy

Each case should be taught in two passes.

## Pass 1 — Learner decides

Stop at:

> **What evidence should we generate, and how?**

The learner completes the Evidence Selection Card.

## Pass 2 — Reveal

Show:

- expert framing;
- candidate methods;
- selected method;
- limitations;
- evidence standard;
- decision rule;
- hypothetical evidence;
- interpretation;
- decision.

This turns each case into a **simulation of strategic judgment** rather than passive reading.

# 93. Final Conclusion

Cases 01–04 validate the architecture because:

> **the logic remains stable while the evidence-generation method changes.**

The learner therefore learns a durable capability:

> **not “how to run tests,” but how to determine what evidence deserves to be generated for the decision at hand.**

That is the core capability of CORE's Idea → Market system.
