# Why Business Thinking Needs a System

Important business thinking rarely begins with a fully formed idea.

It usually begins with something messier:

- a customer complaint
- an unexpected pattern
- a market change
- a technology shift
- a repeated operational problem
- a competitor move
- a new customer request
- a regulatory change
- a strange observation
- an emerging possibility

The difficulty is that human thinking is not naturally organized for holding all of this together.

We remember fragments.

We jump between ideas.

We interpret before we observe.

We mix facts with assumptions.

We forget why we reached a conclusion.

We find it difficult to explain the thinking to someone else.

That is why people have always externalized thinking into notes, journals, diagrams, records, and structured note-taking systems.

# PHASE 1 — THINK ON PAPER

## Why Humans Externalize Thinking

Before teaching the Business Thinking Canvas™, the learner needs to understand **why the canvas exists at all**.

The basic idea is simple:

> **Thinking becomes easier when you can see it.**

---

## Lesson 1 — Why People Take Notes

People take notes because the mind is not a perfect storage system.

Notes help people:

- capture observations
- preserve information
- remember context
- compare ideas
- organize thoughts
- revisit reasoning
- communicate what they understood
- create a record for future action

Note-taking is therefore more than memory support.

It is a way of **moving part of the thinking process outside the head**.

---

## Lesson 2 — Why Structured Notes Matter

Not all notes are equally useful.

A page of disconnected sentences may preserve information without helping someone think.

Structured note-taking creates relationships.

For example, the Cornell Note-Taking System separates notes into different functions so that information can later be reviewed, questioned, summarized, and recalled.

The deeper principle is:

> **Different parts of thinking benefit from different containers.**

Business problems work the same way.

A business opportunity is not one piece of information.

It contains:

- a situation
- a problem
- an interpretation
- alternatives
- assumptions
- constraints
- implications
- possible actions
- unanswered questions

When all of these remain mixed together in a person's head, thinking becomes difficult to inspect.

---

## Lesson 3 — Why Bureaucracies Have Records

Organizations have another reason to externalize thinking:

> **Decisions have to survive the person who made them.**

Governments, bureaucracies, regulated organizations, and large companies maintain records because decisions often have to be:

- explained later
- reviewed by others
- transferred between people
- audited
- challenged
- implemented consistently
- defended against disagreement

A record creates institutional memory.

This gives us an important principle for business thinking:

> **If an important decision exists only inside one person's head, the organization cannot properly inspect the reasoning behind it.**

A canvas is therefore not merely a worksheet.

It is a **visible record of strategic reasoning**.

# Lesson 5 — What Is the Business Thinking Canvas™?

The Business Thinking Canvas™ is a visual structure for turning an ambiguous business situation into a clearer opportunity and a possible next move.

The purpose is not to predict whether the opportunity will succeed.

The purpose is to help you understand:

- what is happening
- how you're currently thinking about it
- what other possibilities exist
- which choice appears stronger
- what would have to be true
- what should be tested
- what deserves commitment

---

# The Seven Blocks of the Business Thinking Canvas™

## Block 1 — Strategic Gap

What is changing?

Where is the gap between:

**what is**

and

**what could be?**

This creates the context for the opportunity.

---

## Block 2 — Strategy-In-Use

What are we doing today?

What assumptions are embedded in the current way of operating?

What are we treating as true?

This prevents us from discussing a new opportunity without understanding the existing system.

---

## Block 3 — Alternatives

What else could we do?

The purpose is to prevent premature attachment to the first idea.

Generate multiple strategic possibilities.

---

## Block 4 — Better Choice

Which alternative appears most attractive?

Not because it is guaranteed to work.

Because, given what we know now, it appears to deserve deeper examination.

---

## Block 5 — Logic / What Would Have To Be True

What must be true for this choice to work?

This is the bridge between:

**a compelling idea**

and

**a testable opportunity**.

---

## Block 6 — Test

What evidence would increase or decrease our confidence?

What is the smallest useful way to learn?

---

## Block 7 — Commit

What deserves the next move?

The "commit" here is not necessarily a large investment.

It may simply be:

- research
- customer observation
- a conversation
- a prototype
- a pretotype
- a small experiment
- a commercial test

The objective is to identify the **next justified action**.

# PHASE 3 — MAKE THE OPPORTUNITY BELIEVABLE

## From Business Thinking to Stakeholder Buy-In

A well-reasoned canvas is not automatically persuasive.

An opportunity can be logically strong and still fail inside an organization because the stakeholder asks:

- Why does this matter?
- Why now?
- Why us?
- What makes you believe it?
- What are we still unsure about?
- What exactly are you asking me to approve?
- What happens if we are wrong?

The final phase teaches the learner to convert the Business Thinking Canvas™ into a **stakeholder narrative**.

---

# The Communication Problem

Most people present opportunities as:

> "I have an idea."

That forces the stakeholder to evaluate an idea.

Instead, the learner should present:

> **Here is the situation. Here is what changed. Here is the opportunity we see. Here is why it matters. Here is what we still do not know. Here is what we propose to test.**

Now the stakeholder is evaluating a **reasoned opportunity and a controlled next step**.

---

# The Six-Book Narrative Framework

The same six-book architecture used in the commercial system now becomes a communication discipline.

## 1. Murphy's Law — Sell the Cost of NOT Acting or Not Learning

The opening should establish the cost of leaving the situation unresolved.

Ask:

- What happens if we do nothing?
- What opportunity could we miss?
- What existing investment could become less valuable?
- What risk grows if we wait?
- What is the cost of committing blindly?

The stakeholder needs to understand why the issue deserves attention.

---

## 2. Breakthrough Advertising — Sell the Desired State

Describe the better future.

Not:

> "We should launch X."

But:

> "This could allow us to achieve Y."

The desired state should be connected to something the stakeholder values:

- revenue
- margin
- market access
- customer retention
- strategic position
- capability
- speed
- risk reduction

The stakeholder should be able to see **what becomes possible**.

---

## 3. Breakthrough + Your Mechanism — Give the Opportunity a New Way

Explain why this particular path could produce the desired state.

The structure becomes:

**Situation**

→

**Opportunity**

→

**Mechanism**

→

**Expected Outcome**

This is where the canvas's logic becomes useful.

Instead of presenting a generic solution, explain:

> **Why this opportunity creates the possibility of this outcome.**

---

## 4. Ogilvy — Make the Mechanism Believable

Do not rely on enthusiasm.

Show the reasoning.

Use:

- observations
- customer evidence
- existing facts
- strategic logic
- case evidence
- explicit assumptions
- known constraints
- What Must Be True

Then acknowledge uncertainty.

A strong opportunity narrative does not pretend certainty.

It says:

> **Here is what we believe. Here is why we believe it. Here is what we still need to prove.**

That honesty increases credibility.

---

## 5. Wizard of Ads — Own the Opportunity Category

The stakeholder should understand **what kind of opportunity this is**.

Is it:

- a new revenue opportunity?
- a market-entry opportunity?
- a capability opportunity?
- an adjacent opportunity?
- a customer-value opportunity?
- a strategic hedge?
- a new business model?

Naming the category helps the stakeholder compare it against the organization's existing priorities.

The purpose is not clever branding.

It is **mental ownership and clarity**.

---

## 6. Kennedy — Ask the Right Stakeholder for the Right Next Action

The final step is not:

> "Do you approve the entire strategy?"

It is:

> **"Will you support the smallest next action required to test whether this deserves further investment?"**

This dramatically reduces the decision burden.

The ask might be:

- permission to speak to customers
- access to data
- a small research budget
- a two-week experiment
- a prototype
- a pilot
- one internal resource
- one customer meeting

The narrative should make the next action easy to understand and easy to approve.
