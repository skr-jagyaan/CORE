# 0. SCRIPT STATUS

This document converts the current CORE™ ₹39,999 Idea → Market Sprint architecture and production blueprint into **recording-ready lesson scripts**.

The current product architecture defines the product as a hybrid learning + application + personalized strategy program whose purpose is to move one meaningful idea from ambiguity toward a real market decision.

The authoritative learning progression is:

> **Diagnose → Structure → De-risk → Select Evidence → Test → Learn → Decide → Market Path**

The product deliberately does not promise:

- guaranteed product-market fit;
- guaranteed revenue;
- a universal validation recipe;
- a fixed test type;
- a successful commercial outcome.

The product promise is:

> **Bring a meaningful idea into the market, test what matters, learn from real evidence, and decide what deserves the next commitment.**

The scripts therefore preserve the current architecture:

> **Problem → Decision → Uncertainty → Evidence → Method → Test → Learn → Decide**

with the important refinement:

> **Evidence selection comes before method selection.**

A test is one possible evidence-generation method; it is not the universal starting point.

The product's own architecture explicitly states that the uncertainty determines the evidence requirement, and the evidence requirement determines the appropriate method.

# SECTION 1 — SESSION 1: DIAGNOSE

# VIDEO 1.1
## Situation Is Not the Problem

### Target duration
8 minutes

### Intellectual lens

**Dynamic Problems + Bonita Kolb**

### Script

Let me give you a very ordinary business sentence.

> “Our customers are leaving because our competitors are cheaper.”

Sounds reasonable.

But notice what is packed into that sentence.

First:

> Customers are leaving.

That may be observable.

Then:

> Competitors are cheaper.

Maybe.

And then:

> Therefore price is causing the loss.

That's an interpretation.

So we have moved from:

> **what happened**

to:

> **what we think it means**

without noticing the jump.

[ON SCREEN]

**SITUATION**

What happened?

↓

**INTERPRETATION**

What do we think it means?

↓

**PROBLEM STATEMENT**

What are we choosing to investigate?

That's the first discipline.

A situation is not automatically a problem.

The initial problem statement is a frame.

And the frame can be wrong.

[PAUSE]

This matters because different frames produce different actions.

If the problem is price, you may cut price.

If the problem is service, you may redesign service.

If the problem is reliability, you may change the product.

If the problem is changing customer requirements, you may need to change the offer entirely.

So before we ask:

> “What should we test?”

we have to ask:

> **“What problem are we actually trying to solve?”**

Now apply that to your own situation.

Write down:

What happened?

What changed?

What are you currently interpreting?

Do not solve it yet.

Just make the distinction.

### Memory line

> **The situation is what happened. The problem statement is how you choose to frame it.**

### Learner task

Complete the first section of the **Problem & Opportunity Framing Sheet**.

# VIDEO 1.2
## The First Problem Statement May Be Wrong

### Target duration
8–10 minutes

### Script

Here's a useful exercise.

Take your current problem statement.

Now assume it is wrong.

Not definitely wrong.

Just wrong enough to deserve another look.

What else could be happening?

[ON SCREEN]

> **What if the problem is different?**

Suppose repeat orders are falling.

Possible frames:

Price.

Service.

Reliability.

Changing customer requirements.

Relationship.

Competitor capability.

Market structure.

Same situation.

Different problem.

This is why I want you to generate alternative frames.

Not because all frames are equally good.

Because comparison improves judgment.

A common mistake is to find a plausible explanation and then immediately optimize around it.

We ask:

> “How do we fix this?”

before asking:

> “Is this actually the right problem?”

[PAUSE]

For your own situation, create at least three plausible frames.

And for each one, ask:

> If this were the real problem, what would we do differently?

That question is extremely useful.

Because if the answer changes materially, the framing matters.

### Memory line

> **Don't defend the first frame. Compare it.**

### Learner task

Create at least three **Problem Frame Options**.

A problem describes the current state.

An outcome describes the future state that matters.

For example:

> “Inspection failures are rising.”

That's a problem.

[ON SCREEN]

**DESIRED OUTCOME**

What future state matters?

[PAUSE]

# VIDEO 3.2
## Critical vs Fragile

### Target duration
7–8 minutes

### Script

Once you have a list of conditions, do not immediately test all of them.

First distinguish two ideas.

A condition is:

> **critical**

when its failure materially damages the opportunity.

A condition is:

> **fragile**

when it is not supported by adequate evidence.

[ON SCREEN]

> **Critical + Fragile = Dangerous**

A trivial assumption can be fragile and still not matter.

A critical assumption can be well supported and therefore not deserve immediate attention.

The combination we care about is:

> important enough to kill the choice

and:

> weak enough that we do not trust it yet.

That's where we should look first.

### Memory line

> **The dangerous assumption is critical and fragile.**

# VIDEO 3.3
## Find the Critical Barrier

### Target duration
7–9 minutes

### Script

Here is the question.

> **Which condition could most materially change the decision if it failed?**

Not:

> Which one is most interesting?

Not:

> Which one is easiest to research?

And not:

> Which one do we already know how to test?

The question is:

> **Which one could change the decision?**

Suppose a technology has great accuracy but no customer will provide plant access.

Suppose customers love the concept but the economics do not work.

Suppose the economics work but legacy systems cannot integrate.

Each barrier leads to a different strategic response.

[PAUSE]

For your opportunity:

Write the condition.

Then finish this sentence:

> If this barrier fails, ____________________________.

That consequence is what makes the barrier real.

### Memory line

> **Find the condition that could kill the choice.**

### Target duration
8–10 minutes

### Script

This is probably the most important distinction in the entire Sprint.

If I tell you:

> “Every business problem should be tested with five interviews…”

I have already made a mistake.

Because different uncertainties require different evidence.

[ON SCREEN]

### Customer experience uncertainty

Potential evidence:

> customer experience and observed context.

### Behaviour uncertainty

Potential evidence:

> observed behaviour.

### Commercial commitment uncertainty

Potential evidence:

> meaningful commercial action.

### Technical feasibility uncertainty

Potential evidence:

> measured technical performance under representative conditions.

### Regulatory uncertainty

Potential evidence:

> authoritative regulatory information and appropriate interpretation.

Notice what happened.

The uncertainty changed.

So the evidence changed.

And therefore the method changes.

This is the point where CORE becomes broader than an experimentation framework.

Sometimes you interview.

Sometimes you observe.

Sometimes you test.

Sometimes you run a pilot.

Sometimes you analyze documents.

Sometimes you use existing data.

The question is:

> **What evidence would actually reduce the uncertainty?**

> **The uncertainty chooses the evidence.**

# VIDEO 3.8
## What Will This Method NOT Tell You?

### Target duration
7–9 minutes

### Script

This is one of the best protections against false confidence.

Suppose you interview ten customers.

What can that establish?

Maybe:

- how they experience the problem;
- what alternatives they use;
- what they care about.

What can it not automatically establish?

> that they will buy.

Suppose you build a prototype.

It can help establish:

> whether people can use or understand something.

It may not establish:

> willingness to pay.

Suppose you run a technical test.

It may establish:

> technical performance.

It may not establish:

> commercial demand.

Suppose you analyze a regulation.

You may establish:

> what the rule requires.

You may not establish:

> whether customers will pay for a solution.

[PAUSE]

So every Evidence Selection Card should include:

> **What will this method NOT tell us?**

This is not bureaucracy.

It is intellectual honesty.

### Memory line

> **Know what your evidence cannot prove.**

# VIDEO 3.9
## Acceptance Criteria vs Evidence Standard

### Target duration
7–9 minutes

### Script

Now for a distinction that matters especially in technical and operational work.

A test can pass…

and you can still decide that you don't have enough evidence.

Why?

Because two questions are different.

[ON SCREEN]

### Acceptance Criteria

> **What constitutes success for the test?**

### Evidence Standard

> **How strong does the overall evidence need to be for the decision?**

Imagine a technical system.

You define:

> performance threshold;

> data-quality threshold;

> reliability threshold.

Those are acceptance criteria.

But suppose you're considering a large-scale deployment.

One successful demonstration may not be enough.

You might need:
