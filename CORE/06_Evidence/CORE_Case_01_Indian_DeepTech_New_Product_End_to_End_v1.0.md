# CORE™ Case 01
## Indian Deep-Tech Startup / New Product Launch
### End-to-End Evidence Selection Case
### Version 1.0

---

# 1. Purpose of This Case

This is the first worked case for the CORE™ ₹39,999 Idea → Market Sprint.

It demonstrates how the **CORE Evidence Selection Architecture** works when an Indian deep-tech startup has a new product and needs to decide whether, where and how to take it to market.

The purpose is **not** to teach one particular validation test.

The purpose is to demonstrate the sequence:

> **Problem → Decision → Critical Uncertainty → Evidence Required → Evidence Source / Method → Evidence Generation → Acceptance Criteria (where applicable) → Evidence Standard → Interpretation → Decision**

This is a hypothetical teaching case.

No claims in this case should be treated as evidence about the actual Indian automotive market.

---

# 2. Starting Situation

## The Company

A hypothetical Indian deep-tech startup has developed an:

> **AI-based machine-vision solution for detecting defects in automotive-component manufacturing.**

The technology has been demonstrated in controlled conditions.

The founder believes:

> **“Tier-1 automotive manufacturers have inspection problems, our technology can solve them, and they should pay for it.”**

The startup is considering a commercial launch.

---

# 3. The Initial Idea

The initial idea is:

> **Sell an AI-based vision inspection system to Tier-1 automotive manufacturers.**

At this point, this is an:

> **idea / strategic possibility**

It is not yet a validated market opportunity.

---

# 4. CORE Step 1 — Diagnose the Problem

## Initial Situation

The company believes:

> Automotive manufacturers need better defect detection.

We do not accept this immediately as the final problem statement.

---

## Initial Problem Statement

> **Automotive plants need better defect detection.**

This is plausible but broad.

---

# 5. Reframe the Problem

Consider alternative frames.

## Frame A — Quality

> **Manufacturers need to reduce escaped defects and customer rejection.**

This shifts attention from the technology to the consequence.

---

## Frame B — Economics

> **Manufacturers need to reduce the cost of inspection failures, rework, scrap and customer rejection.**

This changes the value conversation.

---

## Frame C — Operations

> **Manufacturers need reliable inspection without disrupting production or creating unacceptable operator burden.**

This introduces operational adoption.

---

## Frame D — Deployment

> **Manufacturers need inspection technology that can work within existing brownfield production environments.**

This exposes a technical/integration dimension.

---

# 6. Why the Reframing Matters

Each frame creates a different strategic question.

### If the real issue is quality:

The evidence must establish quality impact.

### If the real issue is economics:

The evidence must establish economic impact.

### If the real issue is operations:

The evidence must establish workflow and adoption implications.

### If the real issue is deployment:

The evidence must establish technical and integration feasibility.

Therefore:

> **Problem framing determines what evidence later becomes relevant.**

---

# 7. Desired Outcome

The startup does not merely want:

> “Customers to buy AI.”

The desired outcome is more meaningfully stated as:

> **Improve inspection reliability and/or economic performance without unacceptable operational or implementation burden.**

The exact desired outcome should be refined with the target customer context.

---

# 8. What Is the Decision?

The immediate decision is:

> **Should the startup pursue Tier-1 automotive manufacturers as a primary commercial beachhead for this product?**

This is the decision that the evidence must ultimately inform.

---

# 9. What Do We Need to Know?

Potential decision-relevant questions:

### Customer Problem

> Is the inspection problem sufficiently important?

### Current Alternatives

> What do manufacturers use today?

### Value

> Is the improvement meaningful enough to matter?

### Technical Deployment

> Can the system operate within the customer's production environment?

### Operator Adoption

> Will the workflow actually be used?

### Buying

> Can the target organisation buy this?

### Economics

> Is the value large enough relative to implementation cost?

### Commercial Commitment

> Will a target customer take a meaningful next step toward deployment?

These are **questions**, not yet tests.

---

# 10. What Do We Already Know?

The startup should first inventory existing evidence.

## Internal Evidence

Possible existing evidence:

- laboratory performance;
- earlier customer conversations;
- existing pilot results;
- sales objections;
- proposals;
- previous demonstrations;
- technical failures;
- deployment observations.

## External Evidence

Potential external evidence:

- relevant industry information;
- customer-published information;
- competitor information;
- technical literature;
- available market evidence.

At this stage, the team should explicitly separate:

> **Known**

from:

> **Indicated**

from:

> **Assumed**

from:

> **Unknown**

---

# 11. Evidence Status

| Condition | Status | Current interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Technology can detect defined defects in controlled conditions | **Known / evidenced within case premise** | Technical capability has some support |
| Tier-1 plants experience meaningful inspection problems | **Assumed / to be established** | Problem severity is not yet established |
| Existing alternatives are inadequate | **Unknown** | Requires evidence |
| Customers will accept deployment | **Unknown** | Requires evidence |
| Operators will adopt the workflow | **Unknown** | Requires evidence |
| Technical integration into brownfield equipment is feasible | **Unknown** | Requires evidence |
| Customer economics are attractive | **Unknown** | Requires evidence |
| Buyers will make a meaningful commercial commitment | **Unknown** | Requires evidence |

The case is intentionally designed so that technical capability does **not** automatically imply commercial viability.

---

# 12. CORE Step 2 — What Would Have to Be True?

The startup's commercial choice requires several conditions.

Examples:

### Customer

The target customer experiences the problem at meaningful severity.

### Problem

The problem is important enough to justify action.

### Behaviour

The affected organisation can and will change its current process.

### Buying

A real buyer can approve the purchase.

### Economics

The value created is meaningful relative to total cost.

### Technology

The technology performs sufficiently under relevant conditions.

### Integration

The solution can connect to the existing production environment.

### Capability

The startup can deliver and support deployment.

### Ecosystem

Suppliers, systems, standards and other dependencies do not create an unacceptable barrier.

These are the WWHTBT conditions.

---

# 13. Identify the Critical Uncertainty

We now ask:

> **Which uncertainty matters most to the beachhead decision?**

Consider:

| Uncertainty | Consequence | Current uncertainty | Decision relevance | Learning value | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Problem severity | High | High | High | High | Very high |
| Technical integration | High | High | High | High | Very high |
| Operator adoption | Medium/High | High | High | High | High |
| Willingness to pay | High | High | High | High | Very high |
| Procurement process | High | High | High | Medium | High |
| Market size | High | Medium | High | Medium | Medium/High |

The exact ranking must be based on the actual situation.

For this teaching case, suppose the evidence review shows:

> Technical capability looks promising, but there is very little evidence that a target Tier-1 customer will take a meaningful commercial or deployment step.

---

# 14. Critical Uncertainty

> **Will a target Tier-1 automotive buyer make a meaningful commercial/deployment commitment under realistic implementation conditions?**

This is now the focal uncertainty.

---

# 15. Critical Barrier

The critical barrier is:

> **The target customer may agree that the problem exists but still refuse to commit because deployment risk, integration burden, procurement friction or economic value is insufficient.**

This distinction is essential.

The problem can be real.

The technology can work.

Yet the commercial strategy can still fail.

---

# 16. What Evidence Do We Need?

The weak question is:

> **“Do customers like the product?”**

The stronger question is:

> **“What evidence would demonstrate that a target buyer is willing to move toward actual deployment under realistic conditions?”**

Possible evidence includes:

- access to representative equipment;
- agreement to a defined evaluation;
- internal sponsor commitment;
- technical data access;
- request for a commercial proposal;
- agreement to pilot scope;
- paid evaluation;
- another meaningful action appropriate to the target buying process.

The evidence requirement is:

> **meaningful movement toward adoption/deployment.**

---

# 17. What Do We Need to Learn?

We need to understand:

### What the customer says

What problem do they describe?

### What the customer does

What action are they willing to take?

### What constraints they reveal

What prevents deployment?

### What the buyer requires

What must be satisfied before a commitment?

### What the plant requires

What must work operationally?

The objective is not to maximize the number of conversations.

It is to generate **decision-relevant evidence**.

---

# 18. Candidate Evidence Methods

At least five plausible methods exist.

## Method A — Customer Interview

Useful for:

- understanding current problem;
- understanding process;
- understanding alternatives;
- understanding buying process.

Weakness:

> may reveal intention without proving commitment.

---

## Method B — Plant Observation

Useful for:

- actual inspection workflow;
- operator behaviour;
- current process;
- operational constraints.

Weakness:

> may establish the operational problem without establishing willingness to buy.

---

## Method C — Technical Demonstration

Useful for:

- product capability;
- workflow interaction;
- initial technical fit.

Weakness:

> successful demonstration does not prove commercial adoption.

---

## Method D — Commercial Proposal

Useful for:

- commercial seriousness;
- decision process;
- price/value response;
- procurement friction.

Weakness:

> a proposal can be rejected for reasons unrelated to market value.

---

## Method E — Defined Pilot / Paid Evaluation

Useful for:

- technical feasibility;
- operational reality;
- customer commitment;
- implementation conditions.

Weakness:

> more costly and slower.

---

# 19. Selecting the Method

We compare the methods against the actual uncertainty.

| Method | Directness to critical uncertainty | Behavioural proximity | Speed | Cost | What it can establish |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Interview | Medium | Low/Medium | High | Low | Problem, criteria, process |
| Observation | Medium | High | Medium | Medium | Actual workflow / constraints |
| Technical demonstration | Medium | Medium | Medium | Medium | Capability / interaction |
| Commercial proposal | High | High | Medium | Low/Medium | Buyer response / process |
| Paid pilot | High | High | Low/Medium | High | Real deployment / commitment |

### Hypothetical selection

For this case:

> **A structured technical/commercial evaluation followed by a defined deployment commitment request**

may be more decision-relevant than interviews alone.

The precise method must be chosen based on the actual customer and buying process.

---

# 20. Why This Method?

## Directness

It gets closer to the actual question:

> Will the buyer move toward deployment?

## Behavioural proximity

The buyer is asked to take a meaningful action.

## Credibility

The evidence comes from an actual commercial context rather than hypothetical enthusiasm.

## Proportionality

The method is more substantial than a casual interview but less costly than immediate full deployment.

## Limitation

It still may not establish:

- repeatability across the entire segment;
- full market size;
- long-term customer retention;
- every technical operating condition.

Those remain separate questions.

---

# 21. Acceptance Criteria

For the commercial evaluation itself, acceptance criteria may be partly commercial and partly technical.

### Technical — where relevant

Examples:

- agreed performance threshold;
- defined data quality;
- defined integration conditions;
- defined operating duration.

### Commercial

Examples:

- relevant decision-maker participates;
- customer grants defined access;
- customer agrees to evaluation scope;
- customer accepts commercial next step.

The exact criteria must be agreed with the actual organisation.

---

# 22. Evidence Standard

The decision is:

> whether Tier-1 automotive manufacturers should become the primary commercial beachhead.

That decision is more consequential than:

> “Should we continue talking to customers?”

Therefore:

> casual enthusiasm is insufficient.

The required evidence standard might include:

- credible evidence of problem importance;
- credible evidence of deployment feasibility;
- evidence of meaningful buyer movement;
- sufficiently strong economics for the chosen customer context.

The exact threshold is opportunity-specific.

---

# 23. Decision Rule

The rule is written **before** seeing the result.

## GO

Proceed with Tier-1 beachhead if:

- problem is sufficiently important;
- implementation conditions are feasible;
- target buyers show meaningful commercial movement;
- economics remain attractive.

## MODIFY

Continue only after changing:

- customer segment;
- value proposition;
- deployment model;
- price;
- product;
- commercial model.

## STOP

Stop pursuing this beachhead if a consequential condition repeatedly fails.

## TEST AGAIN

Use when the evidence is contradictory or the selected method did not actually address the critical uncertainty.

## ESCALATE

Move to a more serious evaluation if early evidence is encouraging and the next decision requires stronger evidence.

---

# 24. Test Selection Card — Completed Example

## Decision

> **Should we pursue Tier-1 automotive manufacturers as a primary commercial beachhead?**

## Problem

> Inspection failures create a meaningful quality/operational/economic problem that may be improved without unacceptable implementation burden.

## Opportunity

> Deploy AI-based machine vision to improve inspection performance in a defined manufacturing context.

## WWHTBT

> Target buyers experience sufficient pain and can deploy the solution under acceptable operating and economic conditions.

## Critical uncertainty

> Will target buyers make a meaningful move toward deployment?

## Current evidence

> Technical performance appears promising; commercial/deployment evidence is limited.

## Evidence required

> Meaningful buyer/deployment action under realistic conditions.

## Candidate methods

- targeted interview;
- plant observation;
- technical demonstration;
- commercial proposal;
- defined pilot/evaluation.

## Selected method

> **Structured technical/commercial evaluation with a defined next-commitment request.**

## Why this method?

> It creates evidence closer to actual deployment behaviour than an opinion-only interview while remaining proportionate to the current decision.

## Method limitation

> It does not establish total market size or repeatable adoption across the full segment.

## Acceptance criteria

> Defined technical and commercial conditions agreed for the evaluation.

## Evidence standard

> Sufficient evidence that the beachhead has credible problem importance, feasible deployment and meaningful buyer movement.

## Decision rule

> GO / MODIFY / STOP / TEST AGAIN / ESCALATE.

# 25. Hypothetical Execution

Assume the startup conducts the evaluation.

### Buyer 1

Agrees to provide technical data and schedules a plant evaluation.

### Buyer 2

Likes the technology but will not provide plant access.

### Buyer 3

Requests pricing but procurement requires a different deployment structure.

### Buyer 4

Declines because current manual inspection is considered sufficient.

### Buyer 5

Agrees to a paid evaluation subject to defined technical criteria.

These results do not justify:

> **“The market is validated.”**

But they may establish:

- some genuine interest;
- different buying constraints;
- a more promising path through paid evaluation;
- important deployment barriers;
- evidence that the original value proposition may need refinement.

---

# 26. Interpretation

The right interpretation is:

> **The evidence indicates that the opportunity may be viable, but adoption is conditional on deployment proof, procurement structure and economic justification.**

It would be incorrect to conclude:

> “Tier-1 automotive is definitely the market.”

It would also be incorrect to conclude:

> “Customers rejected it.”

The evidence is more nuanced.

---

# 27. Learning Card

## We believed that...

> Tier-1 automotive manufacturers would see enough value in AI-based inspection to move toward deployment.

## We observed...

> Interest varied substantially, and meaningful progress depended on technical access, deployment conditions, procurement requirements and commercial structure.

## From that we learned...

> The value proposition cannot be separated from deployment feasibility and the buyer's internal approval process.

## What changed?

> The commercial proposition needs to sell a credible path to deployment, not just better defect detection.

## What remains uncertain?

> Whether the economics and deployment model can be made sufficiently attractive across a repeatable set of target plants.

---

# 28. Decision

A possible decision is:

> **MODIFY**

Rather than abandoning the opportunity, the startup may modify:

- target account selection;
- commercial proposition;
- pilot structure;
- implementation model;
- proof package;
- pricing structure.

The next evidence-generation activity should be selected based on the new critical uncertainty.

# 31. Teaching Note

This case should be presented in the ₹39,999 product only after the learner understands the Evidence Selection Card.

The learner should be asked to stop at each decision point and answer:

> **“What would you do next?”**

Do not reveal the selected method immediately.

First let the learner choose.

Then show:

> **What evidence was actually required?**

and:

> **Why one method was stronger than the alternatives for this decision.**

This turns the case from passive content into a **decision exercise**.

# 33. Final Teaching Principle

> **Don't ask “Which test should I run?”**

Ask:

> **“What decision am I facing?”**

Then:

> **“What uncertainty matters?”**

Then:

> **“What evidence would actually reduce it?”**

Then:

> **“What is the most credible way to generate that evidence?”**

That is the CORE capability this case is designed to teach.
