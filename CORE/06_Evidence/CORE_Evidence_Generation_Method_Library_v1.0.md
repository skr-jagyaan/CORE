# CORE™ Evidence-Generation Method Library
## Reference Library — Not the Core Methodology
### Version 1.0

---

# 1. Purpose

This library provides a structured reference of possible ways to generate evidence once the CORE process has established:

> **Decision → Critical Uncertainty → Evidence Required**

It is deliberately **not** the CORE methodology.

CORE does not begin by asking:

> “Which test should I run?”

CORE begins by asking:

> **“What decision are we trying to make, what uncertainty matters, and what evidence would reduce it?”**

Only then should this library be consulted.

# 3. The Selection Principle

> **Choose the method that can most credibly generate the evidence required for the decision.**

Do not choose a method because:

- it is fashionable;
- it is familiar;
- AI makes it easy;
- it was successful in another company;
- it is cheap;
- it produces a large quantity of data.

A method is appropriate when it is sufficiently:

- relevant;
- direct;
- credible;
- feasible;
- timely;
- proportionate to the decision;
- capable of producing evidence that could change the decision.

# 5. Family 1 — Existing Evidence Analysis

## What it is

Using evidence the organization already possesses before generating new evidence.

## Appropriate when

The uncertainty may be reduced by:

- historical data;
- CRM records;
- sales records;
- customer complaints;
- service records;
- financial information;
- production data;
- previous pilots;
- failed proposals;
- operational records.

## Typical questions

- What has actually happened?
- What patterns already exist?
- What behaviour has already been observed?
- What did customers previously do?
- Where have we already failed?
- What evidence contradicts the current assumption?

## Typical methods

- data analysis;
- cohort analysis;
- win/loss analysis;
- complaint analysis;
- historical comparison;
- transaction analysis;
- internal document review.

## Strength

It can be faster and more direct than new primary research.

## Limitation

Existing evidence may have been collected for another purpose and may not answer the current question.

## CORE caution

> **Do not treat the existence of data as proof that the data answers the decision.**

---

# 6. Family 2 — Secondary / Documentary Research

## What it is

Using credible external information that already exists.

## Sources may include

- government data;
- official publications;
- regulatory documents;
- public filings;
- industry data;
- technical literature;
- credible reports;
- competitor information;
- customer publications;
- standards;
- market statistics.

## Appropriate when

The question concerns:

- market conditions;
- industry structure;
- regulation;
- technology landscape;
- competitor activity;
- external trends;
- publicly knowable facts.

## Strength

Efficient for establishing external context.

## Limitation

Secondary evidence may not answer a specific behavioural or commercial question.

## CORE caution

> **Market size is not the same as reachable demand.**

---

# 7. Family 3 — Expert Evidence

## What it is

Obtaining evidence from people with relevant specialist knowledge.

## Potential experts

- technical specialists;
- industry practitioners;
- regulatory specialists;
- former operators;
- procurement specialists;
- domain experts;
- channel specialists.

## Appropriate when

The uncertainty concerns:

- specialist knowledge;
- technical feasibility;
- industry practice;
- regulatory interpretation;
- operational constraints.

## Good practice

Ask experts about:

- observed situations;
- actual practices;
- constraints;
- historical examples;
- mechanisms;
- technical requirements.

## Weak practice

Ask:

> “Do you think this is a good idea?”

## CORE caution

Expert opinion is not automatically market evidence.

---

# 8. Family 4 — Customer / Buyer Conversations

## What it is

Structured conversations with relevant customers, buyers or decision-makers.

## Appropriate when

The uncertainty concerns:

- customer problem;
- customer experience;
- buying process;
- alternatives;
- decision criteria;
- procurement;
- organizational constraints;
- unmet needs.

## Stronger questions

Ask about:

- recent events;
- actual purchases;
- current process;
- current spending;
- existing alternatives;
- previous attempts to solve the problem;
- decision process;
- constraints.

## Weaker questions

- “Would you buy this?”
- “Do you like the idea?”
- “Would this be useful?”

These may generate opinions rather than evidence of behaviour.

## CORE caution

> **Stated intention is weaker than demonstrated behaviour or commitment.**

---

# 9. Family 5 — User / Operator Research

## What it is

Research involving the people who actually use or operate the system.

## Appropriate when

The uncertainty concerns:

- workflow;
- adoption;
- usability;
- operational friction;
- training;
- behaviour;
- workarounds;
- operator acceptance.

## Methods

- interviews;
- contextual inquiry;
- workflow walkthroughs;
- task observation;
- diary/log methods;
- usability sessions.

## CORE caution

The buyer and user may be different.

A buyer saying:

> “This is valuable”

does not establish:

> “Operators will use it correctly.”

---

# 10. Family 6 — Observation

## What it is

Directly observing behaviour, workflow or operating conditions.

## Appropriate when

The uncertainty is about:

- actual behaviour;
- process;
- workflow;
- workarounds;
- environmental conditions;
- operational friction.

## Examples

- observe a plant-floor workflow;
- observe customer usage;
- observe procurement process;
- observe equipment operation;
- observe service interaction.

## Strength

Can reveal differences between:

> what people say

and:

> what actually happens.

## Limitation

Observation can show what happened in a particular context without necessarily establishing why or how generalizable it is.

---

# 11. Family 7 — Behavioural / Commitment Evidence

## What it is

Creating a situation in which the relevant party can take a meaningful action.

## Examples

- requesting access to a pilot;
- requesting technical data;
- requesting a sample;
- requesting a proposal;
- agreeing to a trial;
- committing time/resources;
- placing an order;
- paying for a pilot;
- signing a commercial agreement.

## Appropriate when

The uncertainty concerns:

- willingness to act;
- willingness to commit;
- adoption;
- buying;
- commercial seriousness.

## Strength

Behaviour can be stronger evidence than stated intention.

## CORE caution

The action must be sufficiently connected to the decision.

For example:

> agreeing to a 20-minute call

is not equivalent to:

> agreeing to a paid pilot.

---

# 12. Family 8 — Prototype / Demonstration

## What it is

Using a prototype, mock-up, demonstration or early product representation to generate evidence.

## Appropriate when

The uncertainty concerns:

- concept comprehension;
- usability;
- workflow;
- technical interaction;
- value demonstration;
- product experience.

## Possible forms

- physical prototype;
- software prototype;
- mock interface;
- simulation;
- product demonstration;
- sample;
- proof-of-concept.

## CORE caution

A successful demonstration may establish:

> “It can be demonstrated.”

It does not automatically establish:

> “Customers will buy it.”

---

# 13. Family 9 — Technical Validation

## What it is

Testing whether a technology meets defined technical requirements.

## Appropriate when

The uncertainty concerns:

- feasibility;
- performance;
- reliability;
- compatibility;
- integration;
- accuracy;
- safety;
- environmental tolerance.

## Possible methods

- bench testing;
- laboratory testing;
- representative-equipment testing;
- interoperability testing;
- reliability testing;
- performance testing;
- technical demonstration.

## Acceptance criteria

Where applicable, define:

- required performance;
- tolerance;
- duration;
- reliability;
- data quality;
- integration requirements;
- failure thresholds.

## Critical distinction

### Acceptance Criteria

> What constitutes technical success?

### Evidence Standard

> How much confidence do we require before making the decision?

These are different.

---

# 14. Family 10 — Field Test / Pilot

## What it is

Deploying or testing the solution in a realistic operating environment.

## Appropriate when

Laboratory or conceptual evidence is insufficient because the uncertainty depends on:

- environment;
- users;
- workflow;
- integration;
- real-world variability;
- operational reliability.

## Typical outputs

- performance data;
- workflow impact;
- failure modes;
- user behaviour;
- economics;
- implementation effort.

## CORE caution

A pilot should have a defined learning purpose.

Do not run a pilot merely because:

> “We need a pilot.”

Ask:

> **What decision will the pilot inform?**

---

# 15. Family 11 — Controlled Experiments

## What it is

Comparing alternatives under conditions designed to isolate a causal effect.

## Appropriate when

The uncertainty concerns:

> whether changing X causes Y.

## Possible forms

- controlled trials;
- randomized experiments where appropriate;
- A/B tests;
- controlled operational comparisons;
- experimental product variants.

## Strength

Useful for causal questions when experimental control is feasible.

## Limitation

Many B2B, industrial, regulatory and strategic questions cannot be cleanly tested this way.

## CORE principle

> **Do not force an experiment when the uncertainty does not require experimental identification.**

---

# 16. Family 12 — Commercial Tests

## What it is

Using real commercial actions to generate evidence.

## Possible methods

- price quotation;
- commercial proposal;
- pre-order;
- paid pilot;
- paid proof of concept;
- deposit;
- purchase;
- contract;
- renewal;
- expansion.

## Appropriate when

The uncertainty concerns:

- willingness to pay;
- commercial value;
- pricing;
- purchase commitment;
- procurement;
- sales conversion.

## Strength

It can generate evidence closer to actual commercial behaviour.

## CORE caution

The commercial action must be designed so that the result is interpretable.

---

# 17. Family 13 — Scenario / Model-Based Analysis

## What it is

Using structured models to understand possible outcomes under different assumptions.

## Appropriate when

The uncertainty concerns:

- economics;
- scenarios;
- capacity;
- sensitivity;
- investment;
- market structure;
- operational consequences.

## Methods

- financial models;
- sensitivity analysis;
- scenario analysis;
- decision trees;
- capacity models;
- unit economics;
- cost models.

## CORE caution

A model can clarify implications of assumptions.

It does not independently validate the assumptions.

---

# 18. Family 14 — Regulatory / Policy Evidence

## What it is

Generating evidence about external policy, regulation or institutional change.

## Potential sources

- authoritative regulatory text;
- government notifications;
- official guidance;
- standards;
- legal/regulatory interpretation;
- expert analysis;
- affected stakeholder evidence.

## Appropriate when

The uncertainty concerns:

- applicability;
- obligations;
- timing;
- interpretation;
- exemptions;
- enforcement;
- market impact.

## CORE caution

Distinguish:

> **what the rule says**

from:

> **what people believe the rule says**

and from:

> **what the rule will commercially cause.**

These may require different evidence.

---

# 19. Family 15 — Combined Evidence Designs

Some decisions require more than one evidence source.

For example:

> **Existing data → expert evidence → customer evidence → technical test**

or:

> **Regulatory research → customer impact interviews → commercial test**

or:

> **Technical test → operator observation → paid pilot**

The sequence should be driven by the uncertainty.

Do not combine methods merely to appear rigorous.

# 22. Evidence Method Quality Gate

Before running the selected method:

## Relevance

Does it answer the actual uncertainty?

## Directness

Is the evidence sufficiently close to the phenomenon being assessed?

## Credibility

Would a reasonable decision-maker trust it?

## Behavioural proximity

Where behaviour matters, does the method get close to actual behaviour?

## Feasibility

Can it be executed?

## Timeliness

Will the evidence arrive in time?

## Proportionality

Is the effort justified by the decision?

## Interpretability

Will we know what the result means?

## Decision impact

Could the result actually change what we do?

# 23. Method Failure Patterns

Avoid:

## The Favourite-Test Error

> “We always interview customers.”

### Correction

Choose the method based on the uncertainty.

---

## The Convenience Evidence Error

> “This is easy to collect.”

### Correction

Ask whether it is decision-relevant.

---

## The Opinion-as-Evidence Error

> “Five people said they liked it.”

### Correction

Determine what those statements actually establish.

---

## The Synthetic-Evidence Error

> “AI personas all liked it.”

### Correction

AI simulation can support preparation or hypothesis generation. It is not a substitute for real-world evidence.

---

## The Prototype-Proves-Demand Error

> “The prototype works, therefore customers will buy.”

### Correction

Separate technical feasibility from commercial willingness.

---

## The Pilot-without-Learning-Objective Error

> “Let's run a pilot and see.”

### Correction

Define the decision and learning objective first.

---

## The Research-Before-Evidence-Inventory Error

> “Let's conduct a market study.”

### Correction

First establish what is already known.

---

## The Regulation-as-Experiment Error

> “Let's test the regulation.”

### Correction

First establish authoritative facts and interpretation; then test the commercial implications if needed.

# 24. Evidence Strength Is Contextual

There is no universal hierarchy in which one method is always stronger than another.

For example:

### For technical feasibility

A measured technical field test may be stronger than customer interviews.

### For customer motivation

Customer experience evidence may be more useful than technical testing.

### For willingness to pay

A real commercial action may be stronger than a stated preference.

### For regulatory applicability

Authoritative documentation may be stronger than customer opinion.

Therefore:

> **Evidence strength depends on the question being answered.**

# 25. Evidence Levels

The existing CORE distinction remains useful:

## Guerrilla

Fast, inexpensive, directional learning.

Purpose:

> **Does this deserve further attention?**

## Indicative

Stronger evidence sufficient to justify the next level of commitment.

Purpose:

> **Is this promising enough to move forward?**

## Validity

Evidence appropriate to a consequential decision.

Purpose:

> **Is the evidence strong enough for the decision being considered?**

These are **levels of evidence/commitment**, not fixed methods.

Any method family can potentially be used at different levels, depending on context.

---

# 26. Acceptance Criteria vs Evidence Standard

This distinction is mandatory where applicable.

## Acceptance Criteria

Defines:

> **What counts as success for the method/test?**

Examples:

- performance threshold;
- accuracy threshold;
- reliability threshold;
- response-time threshold;
- data-quality threshold.

## Evidence Standard

Defines:

> **How much confidence is required before the decision is made?**

Example:

A technical system might need:

> ≥ defined performance threshold

and the decision-maker might additionally require:

> evidence across multiple representative operating conditions.

These are separate dimensions.

# 29. AI's Role in Evidence Generation

AI can accelerate:

- hypothesis generation;
- research-question drafting;
- interview-guide drafting;
- secondary research organization;
- document comparison;
- transcription;
- coding;
- thematic analysis;
- evidence organization;
- scenario generation;
- test design;
- synthesis.

AI should not be presented as a replacement for:

- actual customer behaviour;
- buyer commitment;
- operator behaviour;
- technical performance;
- regulatory authority;
- real-world commercial action.

Core principle:

> **AI accelerates evidence work; it does not manufacture external evidence.**

# 30. When a Method Should Be Rejected

Reject a proposed method if:

### It cannot answer the uncertainty

> Wrong evidence.

### It produces only opinions when behaviour matters

> Weak evidence.

### It is too slow for the decision

> Redesign.

### It is too expensive relative to the decision

> Find a proportionate method.

### Its results would not change the decision

> Do not run it.

### Its output cannot be interpreted

> Redesign.

### It creates false confidence

> Reject.

# 32. Final Principle

> **There is no “best test” in the abstract.**

There is only:

> **the most credible, proportionate way to generate the evidence required for the decision at hand.**

That is the purpose of this library.

---

# 33. Boundary of the Library

This document does not define:

- universal sample sizes;
- universal confidence levels;
- universal interview counts;
- universal pilot duration;
- universal acceptance thresholds;
- universal statistical methods;
- universal regulatory procedures;
- universal technical standards.

Those depend on:

- the decision;
- the domain;
- the risk;
- the evidence required;
- the consequences of being wrong.

Where specialist standards are required, they should be established with the relevant domain expertise rather than invented by CORE.
